Step 1. Your estimate

The curve below was measured by colliding AdoCbi+ ions with xenon atoms. When a collision delivers enough energy, the cobalt–carbon bond breaks. The higher the collision energy, the more often this happens, which is why the signal rises.

Somewhere on this curve is the energy needed to break that bond. Decide where, and write the number down before you go on.

The vertical axis is the reaction cross section and the horizontal axis is the collision energy in the centre-of-mass frame. The 215 points are taken from Figure S16 of Kobylianskii, Widner, Kräutler and Chen, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2013, 135, 13648.

The ions are already warm.

They arrive carrying some vibrational energy from the source. An ion that already has some needs less from the collision, so the warmest ones break apart at lower collision energies than they otherwise would. This makes the curve start rising too early.

The bond has to break in time.

The ion has about sixty microseconds to fall apart before it reaches the detector. Just above the true bond energy, the energy is shared across all 504 of the molecule's vibrations and rarely concentrates in the cobalt–carbon bond, so the ion usually does not break in time. To see anything at all you have to push the energy well past the true value. This makes the curve start rising too late.

The collision energy is not a single value.

The ions do not all arrive with the same energy, and the xenon atoms are moving as well. This does not move the curve in either direction. It rounds off the corner, so there stops being a clear place where the rise begins.

The first two push in opposite directions, so you might hope they cancel. They do not, because they depend on completely different things: the first on how hot your source runs, the second on how large your molecule is.

Step 5. Where the published number comes from

Since the curve cannot be read directly, the bond energy is obtained by fitting a model to it. The model has two parts.

The first predicts how fast the ion falls apart once it has enough energy. The second predicts what energy the ions actually have. Together the two parts predict what the curve would look like for any assumed bond energy. You try values until the prediction matches the points. The value that matches is the bond energy, and here it is 1.80 eV. Let's pick this up in the lecture.
